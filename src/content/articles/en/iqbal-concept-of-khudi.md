If Allama Iqbal's whole philosophy had to be summed up in one word, that word would be *khudi*. It is also the word most often misunderstood. In everyday Urdu, khudi can suggest ego, pride or stubbornness. For Iqbal it means almost the opposite: knowing who you are, having self-respect, and awakening the abilities that Allah has placed in every human being.

This article explains what khudi really means, the stages Iqbal described for developing it, its roots in the Qur'an, and what an ordinary young person today can take from it.

## What does khudi mean?

In Persian and Urdu, *khud* means "self". Iqbal turned the word into a philosophical term. By khudi he meant the inner reality of a person: what makes each of us distinct, gives us the power to decide and act, and connects us to the purpose of our life.

R. A. Nicholson, the Cambridge orientalist who translated Iqbal's Persian poem into English, explained khudi as "selfhood", "individuality" and "personality".

Khudi is not arrogance. An arrogant person thinks he is greater than others. A person with khudi works to become worthy of the great responsibility Allah has given him. Pride makes others small; khudi makes a person grow.

## Asrar-i-Khudi: where the journey began

Iqbal first set out his philosophy of the self in 1915, in his Persian *masnavi* (long poem) **Asrar-i-Khudi**. Nicholson's translation, **The Secrets of the Self**, was published in London in 1920, and it introduced Iqbal's thought to the West. It was followed by *Rumuz-i-Bekhudi*, in which Iqbal explained how the individual self connects with the community.

One of the poem's foundational couplets reads:

<blockquote class="poetry">
<p>پیکرِ ہستی ز آثارِ خودی است<br>ہر چہ می بینی ز اسرارِ خودی است</p>
<p><cite>— Asrar-i-Khudi (1915)</cite></p>
</blockquote>

In Nicholson's translation: "The form of existence is an effect of the Self, / Whatsoever thou seest is a secret of the Self." For Iqbal, the universe is not a lifeless machine. Everything has a self at its own level, and the human self is the highest and the most responsible of all.

## The three stages of training the self

The most practical part of Asrar-i-Khudi is Iqbal's teaching that the self is not complete at birth; it has to be trained. Nicholson's translation titles the section: "Showing that the education of the Self has three stages: Obedience, Self-control, and Divine Vicegerency."

**1. Obedience.** The first stage is living by a law. Iqbal uses the image of the camel, which carries its load patiently and keeps to its path. For a human being, that law is the Shari'ah. A person bound by no rule is not free; he is a slave to his desires.

**2. Self-control.** The second stage is mastering one's own nafs. Fear, greed and desire weaken a person from within. For Iqbal, prayer, fasting, Hajj and zakat are the means of this training: prayer breaks pride, fasting teaches control over hunger and desire, and zakat loosens the love of wealth.

**3. Divine vicegerency.** The final stage is when a person becomes fit to act as Allah's representative on earth. This is the station the Qur'an announced at the creation of humankind:

> إِنِّي جَاعِلٞ فِي ٱلۡأَرۡضِ خَلِيفَةٗۖ
>
> "Indeed, I am placing a vicegerent on the earth."
>
> <cite>— Surah al-Baqarah 2:30</cite>

The order matters. First a person learns to obey a law, then to master himself; only then is he ready to guide others and carry responsibility for the earth.

## The Qur'anic roots of khudi

Iqbal read Western philosophers closely, but his idea of the self is rooted in the Qur'an. A few verses make this clear.

The dignity of the human being:

> لَقَدۡ خَلَقۡنَا ٱلۡإِنسَٰنَ فِيٓ أَحۡسَنِ تَقۡوِيمٖ
>
> "We have certainly created man in the finest form."
>
> <cite>— Surah at-Tin 95:4</cite>

The value of effort:

> وَأَن لَّيۡسَ لِلۡإِنسَٰنِ إِلَّا مَا سَعَىٰ
>
> "And that a person will have nothing except what he strives for."
>
> <cite>— Surah an-Najm 53:39</cite>

Change begins within:

> إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ لَا يُغَيِّرُ مَا بِقَوۡمٍ حَتَّىٰ يُغَيِّرُواْ مَا بِأَنفُسِهِمۡۗ
>
> "Indeed, Allah does not change the condition of a people until they change what is within themselves."
>
> <cite>— Surah ar-Ra'd 13:11</cite>

And most deeply, knowing oneself is tied to remembering Allah:

> وَلَا تَكُونُواْ كَٱلَّذِينَ نَسُواْ ٱللَّهَ فَأَنسَىٰهُمۡ أَنفُسَهُمۡۚ
>
> "And do not be like those who forgot Allah, so He made them forget themselves."
>
> <cite>— Surah al-Hashr 59:19</cite>

This verse is the key to khudi. Whoever forgets Allah loses himself; whoever turns to Allah finds who he truly is. Iqbal's khudi is not independence from God. It is strength gained through serving Him.

## Action: the real test of the self

Iqbal repeats that the self is built by action, not by ideas alone. In his famous poem *Tulu'-i-Islam* (The Dawn of Islam), published in *Bang-i-Dara* (1924), he writes:

<blockquote class="poetry">
<p>یقیں محکم، عمل پیہم، محبت فاتحِ عالم<br>جہادِ زندگانی میں ہیں یہ مردوں کی شمشیریں</p>
<p><cite>— Tulu'-i-Islam, Bang-i-Dara</cite></p>
</blockquote>

Firm conviction, constant action, and a love that wins the world: these, he says, are the swords with which the struggle of life is won. In the same poem:

<blockquote class="poetry">
<p>عمل سے زندگی بنتی ہے جنت بھی جہنم بھی<br>یہ خاکی اپنی فطرت میں نہ نوری ہے نہ ناری ہے</p>
<p><cite>— Tulu'-i-Islam, Bang-i-Dara</cite></p>
</blockquote>

A human being is born neither an angel of light nor a creature of fire; it is his deeds that make his life a paradise or a hell.

Iqbal's best-known couplet on khudi belongs to the same line of thought:

<blockquote class="poetry">
<p>خودی کو کر بلند اتنا کہ ہر تقدیر سے پہلے<br>خدا بندے سے خود پوچھے، بتا تیری رضا کیا ہے</p>
<p><cite>— Allama Muhammad Iqbal</cite></p>
</blockquote>

"Raise your self so high that before every decree, God Himself asks His servant: tell Me, what is your wish?" This is poetic language. It does not mean that a servant commands God. It means raising one's character, sincerity and effort so high that the servant's wish and Allah's pleasure become one.

## Self and selflessness: the individual and the community

To stop khudi turning into selfishness, Iqbal paired it with *bekhudi*, selflessness. In *Rumuz-i-Bekhudi* he explains that the individual self is completed only within the community, as a wave is a wave only while it stays with the river. A strong individual strengthens the ummah, and the ummah gives the individual purpose and identity. Khudi does not mean "only me"; it means "me, for my community".

## What khudi asks of young people today

A century later, Iqbal's message is still fresh. In everyday life, khudi might look like this:

- **Your own identity, not imitation.** Changing yourself to copy whatever trends online is a weakness of the self. Confidence in your values and heritage is a sign of khudi.
- **Skill and hard work.** A person with khudi earns a place through ability and effort, not connections or shortcuts.
- **Self-respect.** Gains made through haram income, lies or flattery weaken the self. The honest path may be harder, but it builds you.
- **A living link with Allah.** Prayer and the Qur'an are the foundation on which Iqbal's whole structure stands.

## Practical lesson

- This week, choose one habit that trains self-control, such as limiting phone time or praying on time. This is Iqbal's second stage.
- Write down one skill you want to learn in the next three months, and set a fixed daily time for it.
- Reflect on Surah al-Hashr 59:19: which of my habits is pulling me away from Allah, and so from myself?
